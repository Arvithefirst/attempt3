2nd most common cancer in males >65 in the west. Can still occur after TURP because outer zone is left intact

## Clinical Features
1. Same as [[Surgery Notes/Benign Prostate Hyperplasia\|BPH]] (Frequency, Urgency, Hesistency) often revealed on histology of BPH diagnosis
2. Multiple bone pain
3. Hard nodule on anterior rectum with obliteration of median sulcus
4. Bilateral sciatica
5. Acute retention of urine

## Spread
From most to least likely
#### Haematogenous spread
- through prostatic venous plexus
- Usually involves thoracolumbar vertebrae, pelvic bones, femur, scalp, ribs
- Multiple, moth-eaten, osteoblastic deposits
#### Lymphatic spread
- Through internal iliac to para-aortic then left supraclavicular
#### Direct spread
- Medially may involve prostatic urethra
- Upwards can involve bladder cauing painful haematuria
- Can involve seminal vescicles
- Involves denonvillers fascia very late

## Investiagtions
1. Transrectal trucut biopsy
2. Xray bones
3. Serum Acid phosphatase > 1-3 King-Armstrong units
4. Serum Alkaline Phosphatase
>[!faq] Prostate specific Antigen
>Levels >4nmol/ml is suggestive; >10 is suggestive >35 is basically diagnostic
>Also elevated in other conditions (inflammation, infarct.) 
>Also elevated after ejaculation and cycling
6. Abdominal USG
7. CT/MRI
8. Bone scan

## Treatment
depends on staging

### Early malignancy
Refers to T1/T2, N0,M0 with PSA <20
- Radical prostatectomy if life expectancy >10 years (may damage dorsal venous plexus)
- Radical radiotherapy

### Late malignancy
- Low bilateral orchidectomy
- Anti-androgen therapy(stilboestrol)
- Radical Radiotherapy (Local or hemobody)
- Palliative care with local radiotherapy or chomotherapeutics like mitomycin
